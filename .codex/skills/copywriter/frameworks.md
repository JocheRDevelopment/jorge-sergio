# Copywriting Frameworks

Frameworks are scaffolds, not scripts. Pick one based on the reader's awareness stage and offer complexity. Then forget the framework — the page should read like the writer cared, not like they followed a recipe.

## Quick selection guide

| Situation | Use |
|-----------|-----|
| Reader feels acute, conscious pain | **PAS** |
| Reader knows solutions exist, comparing options | **AIDA** |
| Long-form, high-trust offer (coaching, B2B demo page, course) | **PASTOR** |
| Transformation pitch / hero section | **BAB** |
| Hero section, need to compress everything | **4 Ps** |
| Case-study-led page | **Star-Story-Solution** |
| Reader needs educating before they can buy | **ACCA** |
| Single section translating features → outcomes | **FAB** |

## AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

E. St. Elmo Lewis, 1898. The default for landing pages and ads. Gary Halbert's go-to formula.

**When to use:** Solution-aware traffic. Most landing pages. Most ads.

**Structure:**
- **Attention** — Stop the scroll. Headline calls out the reader's situation or pain.
- **Interest** — Show why this matters. Hook the curiosity.
- **Desire** — Make them want the after-state. Proof, social, specifics.
- **Action** — Tell them exactly what to do. One CTA.

**Example (SaaS):**
- A: "Your team wastes 6 hours a week on status meetings."
- I: "Linear replaces those with one async page everyone reads."
- D: "Teams at Vercel and Ramp ship 2× faster after switching."
- A: "Start my free trial."

## PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solution

Dan Kennedy: "the most reliable copywriting formula for sales ever invented."

**When to use:** Pain is acute and conscious. Problem-aware audience. Don't use on most-aware buyers — they've already lived the pain, agitating it annoys them.

**Structure:**
- **Problem** — State the reader's pain back to them in their words.
- **Agitate** — Make the consequences concrete. What does this pain cost?
- **Solution** — Introduce the way out.

**Example:**
> Your landing page converts at 2%. Every wasted click on a $4 CPC ad burns $200/day — over a year, that's the cost of a senior hire you never made. Unbounce Smart Builder lifts conversion an average of 30%.

**Failure mode:** Stopping at agitate. PAS only works if the Solution lands clean and feels inevitable after the agitation.

## PASTOR — Problem, Amplify, Story, Transformation, Offer, Response

Ray Edwards. Long-form's workhorse.

**When to use:** Long-form sales pages, coaching/info products, B2B SaaS demo pages where trust needs depth. Don't use for simple offers — it's overkill.

**Structure:**
- **Problem** — Name the pain.
- **Amplify** — Consequences of doing nothing. The cost of the status quo.
- **Story** — A real person who lived this. Their before-state, the moment of change, the after-state.
- **Transformation** — Paint the after-state the reader will live in.
- **Offer** — The specific thing, the specific terms, the specific price.
- **Response** — Exactly what to do next.

**Why it works:** The Story section earns the trust that long-form needs. Without a real, specific customer in the middle, PASTOR collapses into long PAS.

## FAB — Features, Advantages, Benefits

Section-level translator from spec sheet to human outcome.

**When to use:** Inside a feature block, a benefits grid, or a comparison table. Not as a whole-page structure.

**Pattern:** Feature → So what? → Advantage → So what? → Benefit → So what? → Emotional payoff.

**Example:**
- Feature: 256-bit AES encryption
- → "Your data can't be read if intercepted"
- → "You can email client contracts without losing sleep over a breach"

Walk the chain until you hit something the reader actually cares about. Stop at the first link that makes them feel something.

## BAB — Before, After, Bridge

Transformation copy in three beats.

**When to use:** Hero sections. Email openers. Section transitions. Anywhere you want to show "what life looks like with our thing."

**Structure:**
- **Before** — The reader's current frustrating reality.
- **After** — The future they want.
- **Bridge** — Your product as the path.

**Example:**
> **Before:** Spreadsheet chaos at month-end. **After:** Books closed in 3 days. **Bridge:** Puzzle, the AI-native ledger built for finance teams.

The compression is the point. BAB doesn't argue — it shows two pictures and names the path between them.

## 4 Ps — Picture, Promise, Proof, Push

Henry Hoke. A hero-section recipe.

**When to use:** Above-the-fold copy where you have ~50 words to do everything.

**Structure:**
- **Picture** — Paint the desired outcome. ("Imagine shipping your next release without a single status meeting.")
- **Promise** — State what the product delivers. ("Linear's async-by-default workflow makes that the norm.")
- **Proof** — Quick credibility. ("Used by 10,000+ engineering teams including Vercel, Ramp, Cash App.")
- **Push** — The CTA. ("Start free.")

## Star-Story-Solution

**When to use:** Case-study-led pages, customer-success stories, founder-led narratives.

**Structure:**
- **Star** — Introduce the hero (usually a customer, sometimes the founder).
- **Story** — Their journey: before-state, struggle, turning point.
- **Solution** — How your product was the catalyst. The reader projects themselves onto the Star.

The customer is the hero. You are the guide. (Donald Miller's StoryBrand framework is the same idea in different vocabulary.)

## ACCA — Awareness, Comprehension, Conviction, Action

**When to use:** Reader needs education before they can buy. New category. Technical product. Audience that's unaware or problem-aware.

**Structure:**
- **Awareness** — Make them aware a problem (or opportunity) exists.
- **Comprehension** — Explain how it works, what's at stake.
- **Conviction** — Build belief in your specific solution.
- **Action** — CTA.

Slower than AIDA. Necessary when the reader can't act on AIDA's "Desire" yet because they don't yet understand the category.

## The 4 Cs — Clear, Concise, Compelling, Credible

Not a structure — a quality checklist. After drafting, run each line through:

- **Clear:** Can a smart 8th grader understand it on first read?
- **Concise:** Is every word necessary?
- **Compelling:** Does it make them want to read the next line?
- **Credible:** Is there proof, or is this just claims?

If any answer is no, rewrite.

## Joanna Wiebe's "Work Backwards" formula

Not a structure but a method that pairs with any framework above.

1. Write the CTA button copy first ("I want to ___").
2. Write only the copy needed to make someone click that button.
3. Cut anything that doesn't drive toward the click.

This is the single most useful constraint when a draft is bloated. Re-read with the question "does this make them more likely to click?" and cut everything that doesn't.

## Joe Sugarman's Slippery Slide

Also not a structure — a principle that should be true of any structure you use.

> Your readers should be so compelled to read your copy that they cannot stop reading until they read all of it as if sliding down a slippery slide.
> — Sugarman, Axiom 6

Every element exists to make you read the next one. The headline sells the first sentence. The first sentence sells the second. And so on, down to the CTA.

**Techniques that grease the slide:**
- Open loops ("I'll tell you why in a second.")
- Curiosity gaps ("There's one thing most teams miss...")
- "But there's more"-style continuations
- Bucket brigades ("Here's the thing." "But wait —" "It gets better.")
- Personal stories that interrupt argument
- Short sentences after long ones, like punches

When choosing between two drafts, the one that's harder to stop reading wins, regardless of framework.

## How to actually use these

1. Diagnose the reader's awareness stage first. (See `awareness-and-sophistication.md`.)
2. Pick the framework that fits (use the table above).
3. Write the CTA first (Wiebe).
4. Draft using the framework's beats as section guides.
5. Forget the framework and edit so it reads like prose, not a recipe.
6. Apply the Slippery Slide test to every sentence transition.
