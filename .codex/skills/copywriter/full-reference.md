# Full Reference: Landing Page Copywriting

The complete source document this skill was built from. Use this when the focused reference files don't cover what you need — for attribution, expert quotes, specific stats with sources, edge-case topics, or anything the focused refs treat too briefly.

For day-to-day work, the focused references (frameworks, headlines, audit, editing, persuasion, VoC, awareness) will usually be enough. Read this when you specifically need depth.

## TL;DR

High-converting landing pages combine a proven framework (PAS, AIDA, PASTOR, BAB) with a disciplined anatomy: benefit-led headline → subhead → hero visual → primary CTA → social proof → benefits → objections → repeat CTA. Per Unbounce's Q4 2024 Conversion Benchmark Report (41,000 pages, 57M conversions), the cross-industry median is 6.6%; the top 25% of pages convert at 10%+ and the top 10% at 11.45%+.

The single biggest lever is matching your message to the reader's awareness level (Eugene Schwartz's 5 stages) in their exact language, harvested through voice-of-customer research rather than authored from your own assumptions.

CTAs, headlines, and microcopy carry disproportionate weight. Michael Aagaard's Unbounce test showed "Start my free 30-day trial" beat "Start your free 30-day trial" by 90% clicks; specific guarantees and reassurance microcopy ("No credit card required") consistently lift conversion.

## Key Findings

- Frameworks are scaffolds, not scripts. AIDA and PAS cover most landing pages; PASTOR adds narrative depth for high-consideration offers; BAB and FAB are best for in-section copy.
- Structure beats creativity. Unbounce's 2024 benchmark gap — median 6.6% vs. top 10% at 11.45%+ — comes mostly from a disciplined anatomy and one conversion goal per page, not from copywriting flourish.
- Specificity is the highest-ROI edit. Claude Hopkins' century-old rule still holds: "78-second shave" beats "fastest shave"; "Save 4.2 hours/week" beats "save time."
- Customer language outperforms clever language. Joanna Wiebe's rehab landing-page test replaced the control headline "Your Addiction Ends Here" with an Amazon review-mined "If you think you need rehab, you do" and reported ">400% more clicks on the orange button… and >20% more lead-gen form submits on the next page."
- First-person, benefit-led CTAs win. Aagaard: "Start my free 30-day trial" beat "Start your free 30-day trial" by 90% CTR.
- Forms: every field has a cost. HubSpot's 40,000-page Zarrella-era analysis showed 3 fields convert at ~25%, with sharp drops as fields are added.
- AI personalization is now table stakes. HubSpot's analysis of 330,000+ CTAs over six months: personalized CTAs convert 202% better than basic CTAs. McKinsey's Next in Personalization research finds personalization drives a 5–15% revenue lift (range 5–25%) for most companies — not a uniform conversion lift, but a meaningful directional gain.

## 1. Foundational Copywriting Frameworks

**AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action** (E. St. Elmo Lewis, 1898). The default for landing pages and ads. Gary Halbert called it his go-to formula in The Boron Letters.

Example (SaaS):
- A: "Your team wastes 6 hours a week on status meetings."
- I: "Linear replaces those with one async page everyone reads."
- D: "Teams at Vercel and Ramp ship 2× faster after switching."
- A: "Start my free trial."

**PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solution.** Dan Kennedy called PAS "the most reliable copywriting formula for sales ever invented." Best when pain is acute and conscious.

Example: "Your landing page converts at 2%. Every wasted click on a $4 CPC ad burns $200/day. Unbounce Smart Builder lifts conversion an average of 30%."

**PASTOR — Problem, Amplify, Story, Transformation, Offer, Response** (Ray Edwards). Best for long-form sales pages, coaching/info products, and SaaS demo pages where trust needs depth.

**FAB — Features, Advantages, Benefits.** Used at the section level to translate specs into outcomes. Apply the "So what?" test recursively: Feature → So what? → Benefit → So what? → Ultimate emotional payoff.

Example: Feature: "256-bit AES encryption." → "Your data can't be read if intercepted." → "You can email client contracts without losing sleep over a breach."

**BAB — Before, After, Bridge.** Powerful for transformation-led copy.

Example: "Before: Spreadsheet chaos at month-end. After: Books closed in 3 days. Bridge: Puzzle, the AI-native ledger built for finance teams."

**4 Ps — Picture, Promise, Proof, Push.** Henry Hoke's framework; great for hero sections.

**Star-Story-Solution** — Open with a hero (often the customer), tell their journey, position the product as the catalyst. Strong for case-study-led pages.

**The 4 Cs — Clear, Concise, Compelling, Credible.** A copy-quality checklist, not a structure.

**ACCA — Awareness, Comprehension, Conviction, Action.** Use when education is required before the buying decision.

**Joanna Wiebe's "Work Backwards" formula:** Start with the CTA button copy. Then write only the copy needed to make someone click that button. Nothing else makes the page.

**Joe Sugarman's Slippery Slide:** Every element exists to make you read the next one. The headline sells the first sentence; the first sentence sells the second; and so on, down to the CTA. Use open loops, "but there's more," personal stories, and curiosity gaps. Sugarman's Axiom 6: "Your readers should be so compelled to read your copy that they cannot stop reading until they read all of it as if sliding down a slippery slide."

## 2. Landing Page Structure & Anatomy

**Above-the-fold essentials (4 elements):**

1. Headline — benefit-led, message-matched to the ad or email that drove the click.
2. Subheadline — specifies how and adds a concrete proof point.
3. Hero visual — product UI, transformation image, or short looping video; aspirational, showing the desired future state.
4. Primary CTA — one dominant action. A secondary CTA ("Watch the demo," "See pricing") may exist but visually subdued.

**Optimal section order (the "80/20" structure):**

1. Hero (headline + subhead + CTA + visual)
2. Social proof bar (logos, "Trusted by 12,000+ teams")
3. The problem (call out the reader's pain)
4. The solution / how it works (3–5 benefit blocks)
5. Detailed features framed as outcomes
6. Testimonials / case studies / numbers
7. Objections (FAQ, guarantee, risk reversal)
8. Pricing or final offer details
9. Final CTA, often with urgency/scarcity reminder
10. Trust footer (security, privacy, contact)

**Long-form vs. short-form:**

- Short-form when prospects are most aware or product aware, traffic is warm, or the offer is simple/low-commitment.
- Long-form when prospects are unaware/problem aware, the price is high, the product is novel, or skepticism is significant. Schwartz's principle: the colder and less-aware the audience, the longer the journey.

**Social proof — types and placement:**

- Logo bar: immediately below the hero
- Numerical proof: "12,847 teams shipped faster this month"
- Testimonials with photo + role: paired with the benefit block they validate
- Case studies / mini-stories: mid-page, after features
- Star ratings / third-party badges (G2, Trustpilot): near the CTA
- Press mentions: footer or near hero

**CTA frequency:** One primary CTA repeated 2–4 times (hero, mid-page, pricing/offer, final). Pages with 5+ unique CTAs convert significantly worse than focused pages.

**Trust signals:** Security badges, SOC 2 / GDPR mentions, privacy assurances, founder photo + name, real customer names and companies (no "John D., NY"), specific numbers over round numbers.

## 3. Headlines

**Michael Masterson's 4 U's** (priority order for landing pages): Useful, Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific. Rate each candidate 1–4 on each U; if it doesn't score ≥3 on at least three, rewrite. Ogilvy: "On the average, five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. When you have written your headline, you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar."

**Proven headline formulas:**

- How-to: "How to publish a podcast in 10 minutes without editing audio."
- Listicle: "7 ways to cut your AWS bill by 40%."
- Question: "Are you making these [N] mistakes with [topic]?"
- Problem→Promise: "Stop guessing. Start knowing."
- Specific number + outcome: "Land 3 qualified demos a week — guaranteed."
- Reverse testimonial: swipe a customer's exact phrase (Wiebe's rehab page swiped "If you think you need rehab, you do" from an Amazon book review).
- "Imagine if…" — paints the after-state directly.
- Negative/counterintuitive: "Don't hire another developer until you read this."

**Ogilvy's headline rules:** 6–12 words; clear benefit; brand name when possible; honest; no clever puns the reader has to decode.

**Subheadline best practices:** Expand on the promise; introduce the how; add a concrete number ("100% confidence," "in under 5 minutes"); never repeat the headline verbatim.

**Common headline mistakes:**

- Cleverness over clarity (puns, alliteration that obscures the promise)
- Talking about the company ("Welcome to our new platform")
- Vague platitudes ("Innovative solutions for the modern business")
- No specificity ("Save time and money")
- Buzzword soup ("Synergistic, AI-powered, next-gen workflows")

**Testing approach:** Test message angles, not word substitutions — benefit-led vs. curiosity-led vs. problem-led. That's where lift lives.

## 4. CTAs (Calls to Action)

**Button copy formulas:**

- Complete the sentence "I want to ______." (Joanna Wiebe)
- Lead with a strong verb: Get, Start, Claim, Unlock, See, Build, Try, Send, Download.
- First-person "my/me" not "your/you": Aagaard's Unbounce test showed a 90% CTR lift switching "your → my."
- State the value, not the work: "Watch the video," not "Sign in to watch."
- Avoid generic "Submit," "Click Here," "Learn More" when specific copy will do.

**CTA design:**

- High color contrast (4.5:1 minimum). Orange and green often win A/B tests because they contrast — the brand-specific palette matters more than any "magic" hue.
- Largest clickable element in its visual context; surrounded by generous whitespace.
- One primary CTA per "screen" of scroll; secondary CTAs subdued.

**Placement strategies:**

- Above the fold (for ready-to-act / most-aware visitors)
- After the value proposition is made (often mid-page)
- Near page end (for methodical readers)
- Counter-intuitive finding (Aagaard): Moving a form below the fold on a delivery-service PPC page beat the above-fold control by 304% because the page first needed to make its case.

**Microcopy around CTAs (click triggers):**

- "No credit card required" — Totango's 20,000-visitor A/B test showed this microcopy outperformed credit-card-required on 90-day LTV conversion.
- "Cancel anytime"
- "Takes less than 60 seconds"
- "Join 12,847 marketers"
- "30-day money-back guarantee"

**Privacy microcopy is tricky.** Aagaard's tests: "100% privacy — we will never spam you!" decreased sign-ups by 18.7% (the word "spam" itself created anxiety). Rewriting to "We guarantee 100% privacy. Your information will not be shared" lifted sign-ups by 19.5%. Frame privacy as a guarantee, not a denial of misuse.

**Common CTA mistakes:**

- Introducing work ("Fill out form to download")
- Competing equal-weight CTAs that cause decision paralysis
- Low-contrast or hidden button
- No clear next-step expectation ("Get started" → started doing what?)
- Cute over clear ("Enjoy" instead of "Buy now")

## 5. Persuasion & Psychology

**Cialdini's 6 (now 7) principles on landing pages:**

| Principle | Landing-page application |
|-----------|--------------------------|
| Reciprocity | Free download, trial, audit, valuable ungated content |
| Commitment / Consistency | Micro-commitments, multi-step forms, "checked" defaults, value-first quizzes |
| Social Proof | Reviews, logos, customer counts, testimonials with faces |
| Authority | Bylines, certifications, press logos, expert endorsements |
| Liking | Photos of real people, conversational tone, shared values |
| Scarcity | Limited seats, deadline timers, low-stock notices (only when real) |
| Unity (added 2016) | "Built for [in-group]," community-led copy |

**Key cognitive biases to design for:**

- **Loss aversion:** Frame losses ("Stop wasting $40K/year") — roughly twice as motivating as equivalent gains (Kahneman/Tversky).
- **Anchoring:** Show the higher "value" or strikethrough price first on pricing pages.
- **Status quo bias:** Risk reversal (guarantees, free trials) overcomes it.
- **Decoy effect:** Three-tier pricing where the middle plan is the deliberate "winner."
- **Endowment effect:** "Reserve your spot," "Your dashboard is ready."

**Emotional vs. rational:** Per Harvard's Gerald Zaltman (How Customers Think, and Mahoney & Zaltman, "The Subconscious Mind of the Consumer," HBR, Jan 13, 2003): "95% of our purchase decision making takes place in the subconscious mind." Lead with the emotional outcome (feeling, identity, transformation), then back it with logical proof (numbers, integrations, security).

**Storytelling:** Customer hero > brand hero. Use one-paragraph mini-stories between feature blocks: a real customer, a concrete before-state, the moment of change, the after-state.

## 6. Voice, Tone & Style

- **"You" outweighs "we."** A scanned page should have roughly 3–5× more "you/your" than "we/our/us."
- **Conversational > formal.** Read your copy aloud; if you'd never say it that way, rewrite. Ogilvy: "Write your copy in the colloquial language which your customers use in everyday conversation."
- **Reading level:** Aim for Flesch Reading Ease 60–80 (roughly 7th–8th grade) for landing pages; WCAG 2.0 AAA criterion 3.1.5 calls for ≤ lower secondary education (Flesch-Kincaid Grade 7–9). Tools: Hemingway App, Yoast SEO, Readable.com.
- **Active voice, mostly.** "Cut your billing time in half" beats "Billing time can be reduced."
- **Sentence rhythm:** Mix short punchy sentences with the occasional longer one (Gary Provost's principle: vary length to keep music in the prose).
- **Cut jargon ruthlessly** unless your audience explicitly uses it (e.g., "ROAS" is fine for paid-media buyers; "synergize" is never fine).

## 7. Benefits-Driven Writing

**The "So what?" test:** for every feature claim, ask "So what?" until you reach a concrete emotional or functional outcome.

- Feature: "Live collaboration."
- So what? → "Two people can edit at once."
- So what? → "You stop emailing v2_final_FINAL.docx around."
- So what? → "You ship the deck without the 11pm panic."

**Pair emotional and functional benefits.** Functional: "Save 4 hours/week." Emotional: "Leave work on time. Be there for bedtime."

**Outcome-focused language:** Lead with the verb the customer wants to perform on their world ("Close more deals," "Sleep through the night," "Launch in days, not months").

## 8. Common Landing-Page Copy Mistakes

- Generic value propositions ("World-class solutions for modern teams")
- Feature dumping without translating to outcomes
- Weak/vague CTAs ("Submit," "Learn more")
- Talking about the company ("We were founded in 2015…")
- Buzzword overload
- Wall-of-text paragraphs that defeat the F-pattern scan (per NN/g eye-tracking, users read ~20–28% of words on an average page)
- Multiple competing CTAs with equal visual weight
- No social proof above the fold
- No specificity — round numbers, vague claims, no proof
- Message mismatch between ad and landing page headline

## 9. Modern Considerations (2024–2026)

**SEO copywriting integration:** Write for humans first, then optimize. Use the target keyword in the H1, first 100 words, one H2, and meta description — but never sacrifice clarity for stuffing. With Answer Engine Optimization (AEO) growing, structure FAQs with FAQPage schema so AI assistants surface your answers.

**AI and personalization.** HubSpot's six-month analysis of 330,000+ CTAs found personalized CTAs convert 202% better than basic CTAs. McKinsey's Next in Personalization research shows personalization drives a 5–15% revenue lift for most companies (range 5–25%); fast-growing companies derive 40% more of their revenue from personalization than their slower-growing peers. Practical applications:

- Dynamic headlines by traffic source / UTM
- Persona-based subheadlines (industry, role, company size)
- Geo-based social proof
- Returning-visitor variants

Tools: Mutiny, Unbounce Smart Traffic, Instapage Personalization, Fibr.ai, Adobe Sensei.

**AI-generated copy is shifting expectations.** Generic AI prose is increasingly recognizable; the moat is voice-of-customer research, specific numbers, and original perspective. Use AI for drafts, outlines, and variant generation; never publish without injecting customer language and concrete proof.

**Accessibility:**

- Flesch-Kincaid Grade ≤9 (WCAG 2.0 AAA, 3.1.5)
- Sentence-case labels above form fields, not placeholder-only (per NN/g's Katie Sherwin: "Placeholder text within a form field makes it difficult for people to remember what information belongs in a field, and to check for and fix errors")
- Descriptive link text, not "click here"
- 4.5:1 minimum contrast for text
- Don't rely on color alone to communicate state

**Mobile-first copywriting:**

- Headlines under 10 words (often under 7) so they don't wrap awkwardly
- Shorter paragraphs (1–3 sentences max)
- CTAs full-width on mobile, thumb-zone reachable
- Vertical forms, large tap targets, autocomplete enabled
- Lead with the punchline — the second screen is often the last screen

**Voice search & conversational copy:** Mirror how people speak the query ("How do I…", "Best way to…"). Write FAQ answers in 40–60 word direct-answer form.

## 10. Conversion-Focused Techniques

**Specificity and concrete numbers.** Hopkins' rule: replace "fastest" with "78-second"; "international" with "in 52 nations"; "fast delivery" with "delivered in 48 hours or it's free." Hopkins: "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck."

**Risk reversal and guarantees.** Longer guarantees often reduce refund rates because they attract more confident buyers. Templates:

- Outcome guarantee: "If [X] doesn't happen in 60 days, full refund — no questions."
- Keep-the-bonuses guarantee.
- Double-your-money guarantee (high-confidence offers).
- Specific over vague: "No-questions-asked, 100% money-back, 60-day guarantee if [product] fails to [specific outcome]" beats "Satisfaction guaranteed."

**Urgency & scarcity (ethical use only):**

- Real deadlines ("Enrollment closes Friday")
- Real inventory limits ("12 seats left")
- Cohort-based or seasonal limits
- Never fabricate countdowns; trust damage compounds.

**The power of "free."** Gregory Ciotti (Help Scout / Copyblogger) identifies the 5 most persuasive words in English as you, free, because, instantly, new — citing Ellen Langer's 1978 Xerox study where adding any reason after "because" lifted compliance from 60% to ~94%, even when the reason was redundant ("…because I have to make copies").

**Pricing copy:** Anchor with the highest tier first or strikethrough. Frame in usage terms ("Less than $1 per closed deal"). Pre-empt sticker shock with ROI math ("Pays for itself if you book one extra call/month").

**Form copy and labels:**

- HubSpot's 40,000-page analysis (Zarrella era): 3 fields convert at ~25%; conversion declines as fields are added — "multiple textareas have a powerful depressing effect on conversion rates."
- Cut age, street address, and phone unless essential. Zuko's aggregated database shows the phone-number field has a 6.3% field-level abandonment rate (second only to Password); WPForms reports as many as 37% of users abandon forms that require a phone number.
- Labels above fields (NN/g best practice); never placeholder-only.
- Single-column layouts.
- Specific button copy: "Get my free guide" beats "Submit."
- Inline, plain-English error messages, never blame ("Please enter your email" not "You forgot…").

## 11. Pre-Writing Research

**Voice-of-customer (VoC) sources:**

- Customer interviews (gold standard)
- Surveys (post-purchase: "What almost stopped you from buying?")
- Review mining — Yelp, G2, Amazon, Capterra (search competitor and category reviews)
- Support ticket transcripts and chat logs
- Sales call recordings (Gong, Chorus)
- Subreddits and niche forums
- YouTube comments on competitor videos

**Joanna Wiebe's review-mining shortcut:** `site:amazon.com inurl:"product-reviews" "tired of" [keyword]` returns sticky customer phrases by emotional trigger.

**What to capture:**

- Trigger event ("It got really bad when…")
- The pain in their words
- Alternatives they considered
- Hesitations and anxieties
- The moment they decided
- The outcome after purchase

**Competitor analysis:** Look not at what competitors say but at which jobs they're hiring copy to do — what objection is being handled, what proof is being leaned on, what angle they're taking on the same category. Differentiate by angle, not by feature.

**Define one reader.** Write to a single named, fully described person (psychographics > demographics). The "one reader" principle: if you'd be embarrassed to send this copy directly to that person, rewrite it. Ann Handley calls this "relentless empathy" and frames quality content as utility × inspiration × empathy — if any factor is zero, the product is zero.

## 12. Awareness & Sophistication

**Eugene Schwartz's 5 stages of awareness** — the single most important framework for matching message to reader:

| Stage | What they know | What copy must do |
|-------|----------------|-------------------|
| Unaware | Don't know they have a problem | Tell a story, surprising fact, identity hook — do not pitch |
| Problem aware | Feel the pain, no solution category | Validate the problem, name it, introduce the category |
| Solution aware | Know solutions exist, evaluating | Differentiate against alternatives, lead with the solution |
| Product aware | Know your product, not convinced | Lead with specific features, proof, comparisons |
| Most aware | Ready to buy | Lead with offer, price, urgency, simple CTA |

Schwartz: "The more aware your market, the easier the selling job — the less you need to say."

**Market sophistication levels** (also Schwartz, 5 stages): When a category is new, claims work; as it matures, you need bigger claims, then mechanisms, then unique mechanisms, then identity/brand. Don't run a Stage-1 claim ("Lose weight!") in a Stage-5 market.

**Customer journey stage matching:**

- TOFU: educational, problem-focused, low-commitment CTA
- MOFU: comparison, proof, demo/trial CTA
- BOFU: pricing, ROI, "talk to sales" CTA

## 13. Editing & Refinement

**Self-editing checklist:**

- [ ] First line earns the second; second earns the third (Sugarman's Slippery Slide)
- [ ] One primary "you want" per section
- [ ] "You" appears 3–5× more than "we"
- [ ] Every feature has a "so what?" benefit
- [ ] Every claim has proof (number, quote, logo, screenshot)
- [ ] No sentence over 25 words unless deliberate
- [ ] No paragraph over 4 lines on desktop / 3 on mobile
- [ ] No jargon a smart 8th grader wouldn't know
- [ ] CTA copy completes "I want to ___"
- [ ] Headline + CTA could stand alone and still sell
- [ ] Flesch Reading Ease ≥ 60
- [ ] Read-aloud test: no stumble points

**The "bucket brigade" technique:** Short, punchy transitional phrases that pull the reader to the next sentence. Examples: "Here's the thing:" / "But wait —" / "It gets better." / "Now, this is key:" / "Let me explain." / "And there's more." Sugarman calls these "seeds of curiosity"; they grease the slippery slide.

**Cutting unnecessary words:** Strip openers like "I think," "In my opinion," "It's important to note that," "Basically," "Just." Replace "in order to" with "to." Replace "due to the fact that" with "because." Aim to cut 20% on the first revision pass. Ann Handley: "Brilliance — or anything close to it — comes on the rewrite."

**Tools:** Hemingway App (sentence complexity), Grammarly (clarity), Yoast (readability + SEO), Wynter (audience message testing), Readable.com (batch scoring).

## 14. Notable Experts & Their Core Principles

**Eugene Schwartz** (Breakthrough Advertising, 1966): Mass desire already exists in the market; the copywriter's job is to channel it, not create it. The 5 stages of awareness, market sophistication, intensification/identification. Schwartz: "No sentence can be effective if it contains only facts. It must also contain emotion, image, logic and promise."

**David Ogilvy** (Confessions of an Advertising Man, 1963; Ogilvy on Advertising, 1985): "When you have written your headline, you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar." Big ideas, factual specificity, long copy beats short copy when interesting. Ogilvy's "words and phrases that work wonders": Suddenly, Now, Announcing, Introducing, Improvement, Amazing, Sensational, Remarkable, Revolutionary, Startling, Miracle, Magic, Offer, Quick, Easy, Wanted, Challenge, Compare, Bargain, Hurry.

**Joanna Wiebe** (Copyhackers): Founded conversion copywriting as a discipline. Work backwards from the CTA; "call to value, not call to action" — button copy should complete "I want to ___"; mine reviews for sticky copy ("plagiarize your customers"); message mining can move conversion more than design.

**Joe Sugarman** (BluBlocker, The Adweek Copywriting Handbook): The Slippery Slide — every element exists to make you read the next. 17 axioms; the first sentence's only job is to make you read the second. Seeds of curiosity, buying environment, building rapport ("the buyer and seller must vibrate together").

**Gary Halbert** (The Boron Letters): Find a "starving crowd" before perfecting the product. AIDA is the workhorse. Handwrite great ads to internalize them. Be specific. Use parenthesis, conversational language, and transition words. "Tactics are only as useful as the principles beneath them."

**Claude Hopkins** (Scientific Advertising, 1923): Treat advertising as salesmanship in print, not entertainment. Test before scaling (he invented coupon-tracked split testing). "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." Replace superlatives with specific facts. Ogilvy: "Nobody should be allowed to have anything to do with advertising until he has read this book seven times."

**Ann Handley** (Everybody Writes): "Relentless empathy" for the reader is the foundation of all good marketing writing. Start with the ugly first draft (TUFD). Quality = utility × inspiration × empathy. "Brevity loves company. Cut the throat-clearing."

**Robert Cialdini** (Influence, Pre-Suasion): The 6 + 1 principles (reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, unity) — the operating system of persuasion.

**Gregory Ciotti** (Help Scout, Copyblogger): The 5 most persuasive words in English — you, free, because, instantly, new. Cites Ellen Langer's Xerox study where adding any reason after "because" lifted compliance from 60% to ~94%.

**Jon Morrow** (Smart Blogger): Categorized 317+ power words by emotional trigger. Categories to draw from when writing headlines and CTAs:

- **Greed/FOMO:** Exclusive, Limited, Bonus, Last chance, Premiere, Jackpot
- **Curiosity:** Banned, Insider, Members-only, Secret, Spoiler, Sneak peek, Top secret
- **Trust:** Backed, Certified, Guaranteed, No-risk, Proven, Tested, Refund
- **Fear:** Mistake, Risky, Warning, Embarrassing, Toxic, Painful
- **Lust/desire:** Alluring, Captivating, Mouth-watering, Tantalizing
- **Vanity:** Elite, Remarkable, Genius, Fearless, Bold, Epic
- **Urgency:** Now, Hurry, Today, Instantly, Quick

**Michael Aagaard** (ContentVerve, Unbounce): The CRO community's most-cited A/B testing voice. Famous findings:

- "Start my free trial" vs. "Start your free trial": +90% CTR
- "100% privacy — we will never spam" decreased conversions 18.7%; rewriting to "I guarantee 100% privacy" lifted them 19.5%
- Moving CTA below the fold after the case is made: +304% conversion
- Benefit-led button copy: +33.1% clicks

## Recommendations

1. **Start with research, not writing.** Spend at least 30% of the project on voice-of-customer work — interviews, review mining, support-log scraping. Copy then largely writes itself.
2. **Pick one framework per page.** Diagnose the audience's awareness stage first, then choose: PAS for problem-aware, AIDA for solution-aware, PASTOR for high-consideration long-form, BAB for transformation pitches.
3. **Write the CTA first; work backwards.** Every sentence on the page should be necessary to make the reader click that button.
4. **Run a Hopkins specificity pass.** Replace every superlative, vague benefit, and round number with a precise one.
5. **Treat the headline as 80% of the work.** Generate 10–25 candidates; rate them against the 4 U's; A/B test the top two angles (not word variants).
6. **Audit forms ruthlessly.** Default to 3 fields. Justify every additional one with a real conversion-vs-data trade-off.
7. **Build a microcopy layer.** Add risk-reversal lines under every CTA: "No credit card required," "Cancel anytime," "30-day money back," "Join [N] teams."
8. **Read the page out loud on a phone.** Anything you stumble on, rewrite.

**Thresholds to change approach:**

- Conversion below 2% (versus the Unbounce 6.6% median): rewrite the headline + value prop first.
- High bounce, low scroll-depth: above-the-fold message is wrong; check ad-to-page message match.
- Scroll-depth high but conversion low: CTA copy, friction, or offer is the problem.
- Form starts > form completions: shorten the form, add reassurance microcopy.
- Mobile conversion < 60% of desktop: rewrite for mobile-first scan patterns and reduce form fields.
- Conversion already above the 11.45% top-decile threshold (Unbounce 2024): protect the page; shift testing to lifecycle/post-conversion stages and personalization.

## Caveats

- Many CRO stats are single-site A/B test results, not generalizable laws. Aagaard's 90% lift from "your → my" doesn't mean you will see 90% — only that first-person framing tends to win.
- Vendor blogs often inflate or recycle stats without primary citation. The HubSpot 40,000-page form analysis (Zarrella era), HubSpot's 330,000-CTA personalization analysis, and Aagaard's archived ContentVerve tests are the most-cited primary studies; vendor figures (e.g., "AI personalization lifts conversion 40%") often refer to revenue share from personalization (McKinsey: 5–15% revenue lift) rather than uniform page-conversion gains.
- Schwartz's awareness percentages (60/20/10/7/3 sometimes cited) are heuristics, not measured market constants; treat as illustrative.
- Phone-field abandonment figures conflict across sources — Zuko's aggregated database reports a 6.3% field-level abandonment rate, while WPForms attributes ~37% of form abandonments to phone-number requirements. Both are directional; collect your own data when possible.
- Cultural and category context matters: B2B SaaS demands different proof types than DTC e-commerce; long-form rules don't apply to a re-targeted upsell page.
- Use AI tools for drafts and variants, but never publish without injecting voice-of-customer phrasing and concrete proof — generic AI copy is increasingly easy for readers to spot.
- The Ciotti "Yale study" that allegedly ranked "you" as the #1 persuasive word is widely cited but, by Ciotti's own admission, likely never happened — attribute the idea to Ciotti/Copyblogger, not to a Yale study.
