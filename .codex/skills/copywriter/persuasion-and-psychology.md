# Persuasion and Psychology

The principles below are descriptive of how humans actually make decisions, not prescriptive tricks. Use them ethically — every one of them is weaponizable for manipulation, and the trust damage from manipulation compounds far faster than the conversion lift.

## Cialdini's principles, applied to copy

Robert Cialdini's *Influence* identified six universal principles; he added a seventh (Unity) in *Pre-Suasion* (2016). Every persuasive page leans on at least one. The best lean on three or four, integrated naturally.

### Reciprocity

People feel an obligation to give back when they receive.

**On landing pages:**
- Free trial, free tool, free audit, free download
- Ungated valuable content (calculators, templates, benchmark reports)
- Personalized teardowns / "first one's on us"

**Failure mode:** Reciprocity without genuine value. A 2-page "free guide" full of upsells doesn't generate reciprocity — it generates resentment.

### Commitment / Consistency

Once people take a small action, they're more likely to take a larger consistent one.

**On landing pages:**
- Multi-step forms (small first step → larger commitment)
- Value-first quizzes that end with a relevant CTA
- "Checked by default" options (use ethically — only opt-ins the reader actually wants)
- Micro-commitments: "Yes, send me the playbook" before the bigger ask
- Newsletter signup → trial signup → purchase sequence

**Failure mode:** Pre-checked boxes for things the reader doesn't want. Erodes trust and triggers regulatory penalties.

### Social proof

When uncertain, people look to what similar others are doing.

**On landing pages:**
- Customer counts ("Used by 12,847 teams")
- Logos of recognizable customers
- Testimonials with photo + full name + role + company
- Star ratings and review counts (G2, Trustpilot, Capterra)
- Case studies with named metrics
- "Most popular" tags on pricing tiers
- Recent activity ("Sarah from Austin signed up 4 minutes ago" — only if real)

**Failure mode:** Generic or unverifiable proof. "Thousands of happy customers" is worse than "1,847 teams" because the round-number vagueness signals invention.

### Authority

People defer to credible experts.

**On landing pages:**
- Bylines from recognized industry figures
- Certifications (SOC 2, HIPAA, ISO)
- Press logos ("As featured in...")
- Expert endorsements / advisor lists
- Author credentials in body copy ("Sarah ran growth at Stripe for 4 years")
- Awards and third-party recognition

**Failure mode:** Faked or rented authority. Borrowed logos without permission, fake advisor lists. Reader research catches this; trust collapse follows.

### Liking

People prefer to say yes to those they like.

**On landing pages:**
- Photos of real people, not stock
- Founder-led "from us to you" sections
- Conversational tone matching reader's voice
- Shared values made explicit ("Built by [identity] for [identity]")
- Humor where appropriate to the brand
- Vulnerability and transparency ("Here's what we got wrong in v1")

**Failure mode:** Forced casualness. The "buddy voice" reads as condescending when it doesn't match the brand or the audience.

### Scarcity

People value what's limited.

**On landing pages:**
- Real deadlines ("Enrollment closes Friday")
- Real inventory limits ("12 seats remaining")
- Cohort-based limits ("Next cohort: March 4")
- Seasonal availability ("Available through December only")
- Lifetime/one-time pricing offers

**Failure mode:** Fabricated scarcity. Fake countdown timers, "only 3 left" that never decreases, perpetual "limited-time" offers. The conversion lift is short; the trust damage is permanent.

### Unity (added 2016)

People act in alignment with a shared identity.

**On landing pages:**
- "Built for [in-group]" headlines ("Built for finance teams who refuse to chase invoices")
- Community-driven language ("Join 12,847 marketers who...")
- Shared opposition ("Tired of bloated CRMs? Same.")
- Identity-confirming testimonials ("As a founder who's been there...")

**Failure mode:** Pandering. Claiming in-group identity the brand doesn't actually have. Readers from the in-group spot it instantly.

## Cognitive biases worth designing for

### Loss aversion (Kahneman/Tversky)

Losses are roughly **2× as motivating as equivalent gains**. People will work harder to avoid losing $100 than to gain $100.

**Copy applications:**
- Frame the status quo as the cost: "Stop wasting $40K/year on ad clicks that don't convert."
- Frame inaction as the loss: "Every month you wait costs you 12 qualified leads."
- Frame the offer's expiry: "Enrollment closes Friday — and next cohort isn't until October."

Use sparingly and honestly. Constant loss-framing reads as fear-mongering.

### Anchoring

The first number a reader sees shapes how they evaluate every subsequent number.

**Copy applications:**
- Show the highest pricing tier first (or strikethrough the "original" price)
- Anchor against expensive alternatives: "Save the $80K of an in-house hire"
- Show the larger problem before the smaller solution: "Teams waste $1.2M/year on... our $99/mo product fixes this"

### Status quo bias

People default to doing nothing, especially with risk attached. The bias is roughly equal in magnitude to the loss aversion that produces it.

**Copy applications (risk reversal):**
- Money-back guarantees ("60 days, no questions")
- Free trials with no card required
- "We do the migration for you"
- "Cancel anytime, even after the trial"
- Specific outcome guarantees: "If [X] doesn't happen in 60 days, full refund"

Longer guarantees often *reduce* refund rates — they attract more confident buyers. A 60-day guarantee usually outperforms 30-day on net revenue.

### Decoy effect

A third option strategically positioned makes the target option look like the obvious choice.

**Copy applications (pricing):**
- Three-tier pricing where the middle plan is the deliberate winner
- "Most popular" tag on the target tier
- Make the high tier slightly absurd ("Enterprise: starting at $50K/year") to make the middle tier feel reasonable

### Endowment effect

People value things more once they feel ownership of them.

**Copy applications:**
- "Reserve your spot"
- "Your dashboard is ready"
- "Your custom plan"
- Interactive configurators that let the reader build/customize before buying
- Free-trial language: "Your 14-day trial"

### Paradox of choice

More options can reduce conversion. Decision-paralyzed readers leave.

**Copy applications:**
- One primary CTA per page
- 3 pricing tiers (max), not 6
- Default-recommended options
- "Most popular" / "Start here" guidance

### Emotional vs. rational ordering

Gerald Zaltman (Harvard): "95% of our purchase decision making takes place in the subconscious mind." The decision happens emotionally; people then construct rational justification.

**Copy structure that matches:**
1. **Lead with emotional outcome** — the feeling, identity, transformation.
2. **Back with logical proof** — numbers, integrations, security, comparison tables.

Most B2B copy gets this backwards: features first, "value" tacked on at the end. The reader has already left.

## Storytelling on landing pages

Customer hero > brand hero. Always. (Donald Miller's StoryBrand: the customer is Frodo; the brand is Gandalf — guide, not protagonist.)

### The mini-story structure

Between feature blocks, drop one-paragraph stories. The structure is small:

1. **A real, named customer** (with photo when possible)
2. **The before-state** — concrete, specific frustration
3. **The moment of change** — when they encountered your product
4. **The after-state** — measurable outcome

Example:
> "Maya Chen runs growth at Notion. For two years, her team's status meetings ate 6 hours a week — until they replaced them with Linear's async pages. 'I got back almost a full workday,' she says. 'And we shipped our last release two weeks early.' Linear is the only tool that made her async-default vision actually stick."

That's 4 sentences. It does more work than a 200-word feature description.

### The hero must be the reader's mirror

Readers project themselves onto the customer in the story. So the customer's situation must match the reader's situation enough to make the projection effortless:

- Same role/title
- Same company stage
- Same pain
- Same skepticism the reader probably has

A SaaS landing page for a 10-person startup shouldn't lead its case study with a 5,000-person enterprise customer. The reader can't project.

## Ethical lines

These principles work *because* they describe real human psychology. They're also the easy levers for manipulation. Some lines worth not crossing:

- **Don't fabricate scarcity, social proof, or testimonials.** Even small lies erode trust on detection.
- **Don't use loss aversion to invent stakes that aren't real.** "Your competitors are pulling ahead!" without evidence is fear-mongering, not persuasion.
- **Don't pre-check boxes** for opt-ins the reader doesn't actively want. Commitment/Consistency works when the small step is genuine.
- **Don't pretend in-group identity** the brand doesn't have. Unity backfires when discovered.
- **Don't use guilt or shame** as primary motivators on consumer pages. Functional for some narrow categories (charity, health-behavior change with genuine concern); manipulative in commerce.

The five-year test: would the buyer, looking back, be glad they bought? If yes, persuade harder. If no, the offer is the problem, not the copy.
