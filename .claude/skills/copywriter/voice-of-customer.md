# Voice of Customer (VoC)

The single biggest determinant of whether copy converts is whether the words on the page match the words in the reader's head. Joanna Wiebe calls this "plagiarizing your customers." It's the difference between copy that lands and copy that's correct but invisible.

The classic example: Wiebe's rehab landing-page test. The control headline was "Your Addiction Ends Here." She replaced it with "If you think you need rehab, you do" — a phrase mined from an Amazon book review. Result: >400% more clicks on the CTA, >20% more form submits on the next page.

The lift came from one thing: the new headline used the reader's internal monologue. The old headline used the brand's.

## Where to mine VoC

In rough order of signal density:

### 1. Customer interviews (gold standard)

30–45 minute calls with 5–10 recent customers. Ask:

- "Walk me through the day you decided to look for a solution. What happened?"
- "What were you using before? What was specifically broken about it?"
- "What almost stopped you from buying?"
- "What did you tell your boss / partner / team to justify the purchase?"
- "If you had to describe what [product] does to a peer in your role, in one sentence, what would you say?"
- "What's the moment when you'd recommend us to someone?"

Record. Transcribe. Highlight sticky phrases — the ones that make you want to write them down.

### 2. Reviews

Public reviews are VoC at scale, sorted by emotional intensity (5-star and 1-star reviews are the most useful).

**Sources:**
- G2, Capterra, Trustpilot (B2B SaaS)
- Amazon (consumer, books, anything physical)
- App Store / Google Play (consumer mobile)
- Yelp (local services)
- Reddit threads about your category
- YouTube comments on competitor videos

**Wiebe's review-mining search:**
```
site:amazon.com inurl:"product-reviews" "tired of" [keyword]
```
Other emotional triggers to plug into the search:
- "finally"
- "I was looking for"
- "I wish"
- "the problem with"
- "what I love"
- "I almost didn't buy"
- "I was skeptical"

### 3. Sales call recordings

If the company uses Gong, Chorus, Avoma, or any call-recording tool, prospects literally say their pain in their own words. Search for:

- Common objections
- The trigger event that prompted the call
- The phrases buyers use to describe their current process
- How they describe their ideal future state

### 4. Support tickets and chat logs

Reveals what's confusing, what's celebrated, and what makes people angry — all in user language. Particularly useful for:

- Onboarding copy (what confuses new users)
- FAQ pages (what people actually ask)
- Feature page positioning (what existing users praise)

### 5. Subreddits and niche forums

For categories with engaged communities:

- r/[your category] — read the top posts of all time
- Indie Hackers, Hacker News (for technical/startup audiences)
- Discord servers, Slack communities, niche Facebook groups
- Specialized forums (e.g., warriorforum.com for marketers, photography.net for photographers)

### 6. Post-purchase / post-onboarding surveys

Short, fielded at moments of high engagement. Best questions:

- "What's the one thing you almost didn't believe before buying?"
- "What were you searching for that brought you here?"
- "How would you describe what we do, in your own words?"

## What to capture

For each piece of VoC, capture:

| Field | Why it matters |
|-------|----------------|
| **Trigger event** | "When did the pain become bad enough to act?" — drives the problem-section copy |
| **Pain in their words** | The exact phrasing — drives the headline and problem statement |
| **Alternatives considered** | What they almost bought instead — drives differentiation |
| **Hesitations / anxieties** | Why they almost didn't — drives objection handling and guarantees |
| **The moment they decided** | What tipped them — drives the closing argument |
| **Outcome after purchase** | Concrete after-state — drives the benefit copy |
| **Sticky phrases** | Phrases worth lifting verbatim — drives headlines, subheads, button copy |

Build a spreadsheet. Tag entries by theme. Sort by how often a phrase or theme recurs.

## How to use what you mine

### For headlines
Lift sticky phrases verbatim when they're vivid. Reverse-testimonial headlines work because they sound like the reader's own thoughts. (Wiebe's "If you think you need rehab, you do" is the textbook example.)

### For subheads and body copy
Pattern-match across multiple customer phrases to find the consensus pain. If 7 of 10 customers describe the problem the same way, that's your value-prop language.

### For objections / FAQ
The "what almost stopped you from buying?" answers go directly into the FAQ section, often as-is.

### For testimonials
Don't ask customers to write testimonials. Pull the most useful 2–3 sentences from interviews or surveys. Get their permission to use. Real customer language always beats prompted-testimonial language.

### For CTA microcopy
The hesitations from VoC become the reassurance microcopy under the button: "No credit card required" (because that's what 60% of customers said almost stopped them), "Cancel anytime" (because they were burned by a previous tool), etc.

## When VoC isn't available

If the user is writing for a new product, a pre-launch page, or doesn't have customer data:

1. **Mine competitor reviews.** Read 50+ reviews of the closest competitor. Their happy customers describe what they want; their angry customers describe what's broken. Both feed your copy.

2. **Mine adjacent-category reviews.** If you sell project management software, also mine reviews of CRMs and team-collaboration tools — the underlying frustrations overlap.

3. **Read where the audience hangs out.** Subreddits, forums, Twitter/X searches for category complaints.

4. **Write provisional copy, flag the claims that need validation.** Mark every customer-sounding line with `[VoC NEEDED: validate this phrasing]` so the user knows what to confirm.

5. **Never invent customer language.** Generic AI-sounding "Transform your workflow" copy is the failure mode this skill exists to prevent. If you don't have VoC, say so — don't paper over the gap with platitudes.

## Define one reader

Before any VoC work, define **one specific person** you're writing to. Not a demographic — a fully described individual:

> Maya Chen, 32, head of growth at a 40-person Series B SaaS in San Francisco. She has a team of 4 and a quarterly OKR to lift demo signups by 30%. Her last three Webflow contractors ghosted her mid-project. She doesn't trust marketing agencies anymore. She's read every Lenny's Newsletter post on B2B landing pages. She's skeptical of anything that sounds like AI-generated copy.

If you'd be embarrassed to email this person the draft directly, rewrite it.

Ann Handley calls this "relentless empathy." She frames quality content as utility × inspiration × empathy — if any factor is zero, the product is zero.

## Quick VoC playbook (when you have an hour, not a week)

If the user gives you a product/category and zero VoC:

1. **5 minutes:** Define one specific reader.
2. **20 minutes:** Read 30 reviews of the closest competitor on G2/Amazon/Trustpilot. Capture: trigger events, pain phrases, hesitations.
3. **15 minutes:** Search the relevant subreddit or Hacker News for "I'm looking for" + category. Read the top 10 threads.
4. **10 minutes:** Tag and sort what you've collected. Identify the 3 most common pain phrases and the 3 most common hesitations.
5. **10 minutes:** Write the headline, subhead, and first body block using only language from your VoC notes.

Then write the rest. Flag any claim that wasn't backed by VoC for the user to validate.
